Concept and evidence

What Crayola itself says, what our concept proposes, what we only guess, and what is still missing. Company statements are attributed to the company; none is presented as an independent measurement.

The challenge (the "E" in ESG)

What we can say today, with sources

Each card is a statement from a cited page, followed by what it does not show. Numbers in square brackets point to the references.

Product

Super Tips are sold as complete, washable markers

Crayola UK sells Super Tips Washable Markers as a pack of 12 and as a Classpack of 144 in 12 colours, described as washable, non-toxic and suitable from age 3 (Classpack page) [1] [2].

Does not show: how long a marker lasts in a real classroom.

Design

A marker is made of six components

Crayola lists colour solution, a porous plastic tip, a plastic body, a cotton-filament ink reservoir, a rear plug and a cap, and says ingredient detail is proprietary [3]. It also says the components are securely sealed during manufacturing [16].

What this means: today's marker is built to stay shut, so our concept is a redesign of the product, not a way to open the markers already on sale. The pages still do not say which plastic each part uses.

The problem

Recycling mixed-plastic markers is hard in practice

Crayola says its markers contain mixed plastics that make recycling difficult, and advises against pulling out the nib and reservoir to recycle the barrel; only caps may be recycled where facilities accept #5 plastic [16]. It ended its ColorCycle take-back programme (started 2013), citing long-distance transport emissions and contaminated shipments [6].

Does not show: how much plastic each marker contains. For what happens to them in the UK, see the next card.

End of life

In the UK, used markers go in the general waste

The North London Waste Authority tells households that pens "can't be recycled and must be put in general waste" [17]. The alternative is a paid specialist scheme: TerraCycle's pens and markers box costs £145 [18].

What this means: for a household or a school, recycling is either unavailable or a cost. This is the evidence for where today's product sits on the ladder below.

Scale

Two official production figures that disagree

One Crayola FAQ says more than 700 million markers a year [4]; the Canadian site says 465 million [5]. We found no page reconciling them.

Does not show: a single global figure, or anything specific to Super Tips. We quote neither as definitive.

How much plastic

Roughly 5 g of plastic per marker (our estimate)

Crayola does not publish a weight. Tesco lists a 12-pack at 0.1 kg [19], about 8.3 g per marker including ink and packaging. Taking off an allowance for the cardboard wallet, the ink and the cotton wick leaves roughly 5 g of plastic, most of it the cap and body.

Our estimate, not a measurement: nobody has weighed a marker on a scale, and the split between parts is reasoned, not measured. Assumption

Precedent

Crayola has cut plastic in a marker redesign before

The company estimates a Broad Line markers redesign saved about 2 million pounds of plastic against the earlier design [7].

Does not show: any saving for Super Tips or for our Refill concept.

Company claims

Performance, packaging and site energy

Crayola says Super Tips Fine Line write at least 600 feet in its tests [3], Classpack cartons use 100% recycled cardboard, and energy at its own sites cut CO₂e by 82% since 2019 [7].

Does not show: typical life of a marker, the impact of the marker itself, or Super Tips' footprint.

What happens if nothing changes

Regulation

UK Extended Producer Responsibility for packaging has applied since 1 January 2025: large producers must report packaging and pay disposal fees [20]. Northern Ireland also sits under the EU packaging rules [21].

Cuts both ways: refill bottles are extra packaging, so our own concept would be caught by the same rules.

Buyers

The Department for Education expects every school in England to have a sustainability lead and a climate action plan [22]. Schools that buy on those plans will be asked what happens to the markers.

Limit: the guidance does not mention art supplies specifically.

Competitors

Refill systems already exist in nearby categories: STAEDTLER sells refill stations and inks for several of its marker families [8].

Limit: not washable children's markers, so nobody has taken this position yet — but the technology is not exotic.

What this means for the case for change

By Crayola's own account, the marker is a sealed, multi-part, mixed-plastic product that is hard to recycle in practice, and its take-back scheme has closed. In the UK the practical route is the general waste bin. Regulation, school buyers and a competitor are all moving. What we still cannot put a number on is the scale: how many Super Tips are sold here, and the weight of a real marker rather than our estimate — see the open items below.

The proposed shift · Session 1 framework

The R-ladder: where the concept aims

The R-ladder improves on the original 1970s "3Rs" (reduce, re-use, recycle). Companies can adapt it to their category and many pick only a few rungs; recycling should be nearly the last resort, not the default.

More circularLess circular

  1. R1

    Refuse & Rethink

    Forgo certain products or use them more intensively, through innovation in product design, technology and business model.

    Concept: five-part design plus a refill and tip-swap business model.

    Aim
  2. R2

    Reduce

    Make products more efficiently, or make them more efficient to use.

    Concept: may reduce parts thrown away per marker — unproven; needs a life-cycle comparison.

  3. R3

    Reuse

    Product reuse.

    Concept: keep the reusable body and cap in use after the ink or tip is spent.

  4. R4

    Concept: replaceable tip and refillable ink wick, handled by an adult.

Four kinds of statement, kept apart

1 · Facts (company statements, cited)

What the sources say

The cards above. Each is attributed to the page that makes it and carries its limits. Nothing here is an independent measurement.

2 · Decisions proposed by this concept

What the team chose

  • Split the marker into five parts: cap, replaceable tip, ink wick, reusable body, rear plug.
  • Keep the body and cap; make ink and tip the replaceable parts.
  • Sell ink per colour: a single-colour bottle with several doses, so only the colour that runs out is refilled.
  • Put refills and tip swaps in adult hands, subject to safety evaluation.
  • Present the kit as a non-transactional explorer.

3 · Hypotheses

Things we think might be true

  • A body that outlives its ink could stay in use longer.
  • Replacing a worn tip could extend use without replacing the marker.
  • Refill and tip packs could be easier to supply than whole new marker packs, for some schools.
  • Any plastic reduction needs a life-cycle comparison (parts, packaging, transport); being refillable does not prove it.

4 · Pending technical tests

Not yet demonstrated

  • Leaks
  • Bottle connection and dose
  • Closure and loosening
  • Tip retention
  • Ink exposure
  • Small parts
  • Durability
  • Colour constancy
  • Fine and bold strokes
  • Washability on skin
  • Washability on most washable clothing
  • Ink performance vs current Super Tips
  • Child-resistant closure (ISO 8317)

None of these has been tested. Crayola states today's markers are securely sealed [16], so our concept depends on a redesign that has not been built. On the closure: we found no British or European standard that defines a children's marker as "not openable by a child". The nearest applicable route is ISO 8317, the child-resistant packaging test for reclosable packages [23] — it would have to be passed before any such claim could be made, and it has not been attempted.

Handle with care

Claims from the first pitch we are not using as facts

Checked and not confirmed. They stay out of the site until a reliable source exists.

  • Corrected The first pitch said 20,739 primary schools and 8.9 million pupils. The Department for Education figure for state-funded primary schools in England, 2025/26, is 16,712 schools and 4,470,363 pupils [24]. The original numbers covered all schools and all stages.
  • Unverified £1 or £1.50 per refill: a hypothetical price, no refill exists. The first pitch's £3.25 for a pack of 12 is one retailer's price; we now use the average of six UK retailers, £3.58 [15].
  • Unverified That no refillable washable children's marker exists — no exhaustive market search.
  • Unverified That Crayola leads the UK market, or that Super Tips are "the standard" in UK classrooms — no reliable evidence found.
  • Hypothesis Classroom money saved, refill margin, refills per marker and waste reduction — need prices, usage tests and a pilot.
  • Hypothesis That a polypropylene body would be recyclable in practice — composition, separation and local recycling routes unchecked.

The Strategy page still lets you play with the price figures as clearly labelled assumptions, because the brief asks for reasonable estimates.

FAQ

Questions grown-ups might ask

Can I refill my Super Tips today?

No. This is a redesign concept, not a feature of the markers on sale, and this site does not explain how to open them. Please don't try.

Can I buy it?

No. There is no product, price or availability. The kit page is a pretend explorer.

Is it safe for children to refill?

No safety claim is made, and we are careful not to imply one. We found no British or European standard that defines a children's marker as "not openable by a child"; the nearest route would be testing the refill closure against ISO 8317, the child-resistant packaging standard [23]. That test has not been done, so the design intention is all we can describe: refilling is meant to be an adult job.

Will it reduce waste?

Unknown. It needs a life-cycle comparison of parts, packaging and transport. No figures are claimed.

Will the ink wash out?

Washability on skin and most washable clothing has not been tested for this design. Crayola describes its current Super Tips as washable [1]; that is its claim for the current product, not ours.

Who made this, and is Crayola involved?

It is an academic student project. It is not affiliated with, sponsored by or endorsed by Crayola.
