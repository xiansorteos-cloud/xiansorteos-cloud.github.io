Concept and evidence

What Crayola itself says, what our concept proposes, what we only guess, and what is still missing. Company statements are attributed to the company; none is presented as an independent measurement.

The challenge (the "E" in ESG)

What we can say today, with sources

Each card is a statement from a cited page, followed by what it does not show. Numbers in square brackets point to the references.

Product

Super Tips are sold as complete, washable markers

Crayola UK sells Super Tips Washable Markers as a pack of 12 and as a Classpack of 144 in 12 colours, described as washable, non-toxic and suitable from age 3 (Classpack page) [1] [2].

Does not show: how long a marker lasts in a real classroom.

Design

A marker is made of six components

Crayola lists colour solution, a porous plastic tip, a plastic body, a cotton-filament ink reservoir, a rear plug and a cap, and says ingredient detail is proprietary [3].

Does not show: that Super Tips are sealed or impossible to take apart, or which plastic each part is made of.

The problem

Recycling mixed-plastic markers is hard in practice

Crayola says its markers contain mixed plastics that make recycling difficult, and that taken apart the pieces are too small to recycle. It ended its ColorCycle take-back programme (started 2013), citing long-distance transport emissions and contaminated shipments [6].

Does not show: what actually happens to used Super Tips in UK homes and schools, or how much plastic is involved per marker.

Scale

Two official production figures that disagree

One Crayola FAQ says more than 700 million markers a year [4]; the Canadian site says 465 million [5]. We found no page reconciling them.

Does not show: a single global figure, or anything specific to Super Tips. We quote neither as definitive.

Precedent

Crayola has cut plastic in a marker redesign before

The company estimates a Broad Line markers redesign saved about 2 million pounds of plastic against the earlier design [7].

Does not show: any saving for Super Tips or for our Refill concept.

Company claims

Performance, packaging and site energy

Crayola says Super Tips Fine Line write at least 600 feet in its tests [3], Classpack cartons use 100% recycled cardboard, and energy at its own sites cut CO₂e by 82% since 2019 [7].

Does not show: typical life of a marker, the impact of the marker itself, or Super Tips' footprint.

What this means for the case for change

The verified core is narrow but real: by Crayola's own account, the marker is a multi-part, mixed-plastic product that is hard to recycle in practice, and its take-back scheme has closed. The size of the problem for Super Tips (units, plastic per marker, real disposal route) is not yet documented — see the open items below.

The proposed shift · Session 1 framework

The R-ladder: where the concept aims

The R-ladder improves on the original 1970s "3Rs" (reduce, re-use, recycle). Companies can adapt it to their category and many pick only a few rungs; recycling should be nearly the last resort, not the default.

More circularLess circular

  1. R1

    Refuse & Rethink

    Forgo certain products or use them more intensively, through innovation in product design, technology and business model.

    Concept: five-part design plus a refill and tip-swap business model.

    Aim
  2. R2

    Reduce

    Make products more efficiently, or make them more efficient to use.

    Concept: may reduce parts thrown away per marker — unproven; needs a life-cycle comparison.

    Possible
  3. R3

    Reuse

    Product reuse.

    Concept: keep the reusable body and cap in use after the ink or tip is spent.

    Aim
  4. R4

    Repair & Refurbish

    Repair and reuse of product parts.

    Concept: replaceable tip and ink reservoir, handled by an adult.

    Aim
  5. R5

    Recycle

    Processing and recycling of resources.

    Today: Crayola calls recycling of its mixed-plastic markers hard in practice [6]. Where used Super Tips actually end up is not documented, so we do not place them on a rung yet.

    Last resort
  6. R6

    Recover

    Recovering energy from materials.

Four kinds of statement, kept apart

1 · Facts (company statements, cited)

What the sources say

The cards above. Each is attributed to the page that makes it and carries its limits. Nothing here is an independent measurement.

2 · Decisions proposed by this concept

What the team chose

  • Split the marker into five parts: cap, replaceable tip, ink reservoir, reusable body, rear plug.
  • Keep the body and cap; make ink and tip the replaceable parts.
  • Put refills and tip swaps in adult hands, subject to safety evaluation.
  • Present the kit as a non-transactional explorer.

3 · Hypotheses

Things we think might be true

  • A body that outlives its ink could stay in use longer.
  • Replacing a worn tip could extend use without replacing the marker.
  • Refill and tip packs could be easier to supply than whole new marker packs, for some schools.
  • Any plastic reduction needs a life-cycle comparison (parts, packaging, transport); being refillable does not prove it.

4 · Pending technical tests

Not yet demonstrated

  • Leaks
  • Access to the reservoir
  • Closure and loosening
  • Tip retention
  • Ink exposure
  • Small parts
  • Durability
  • Colour constancy
  • Fine and bold strokes
  • Washability on skin
  • Washability on most washable clothing
  • Ink performance vs current Super Tips

None of these has been tested. We have not shown the current rear plug is sealed, that a refill mechanism is safe for children, or that a single-polymer body is viable.

Handle with care

Claims from the first pitch we are not using as facts

Checked and not confirmed. They stay out of the site until a reliable source exists.

  • Unverified 20,739 primary schools and 8.9 million pupils — not confirmed to refer to the same territory, school year or stage.
  • Unverified £1 or £1.50 per refill: a hypothetical price, no refill exists. The first pitch's £3.25 for a pack of 12 does not match the listing found on 12 November 2026 (£3.75 at Sainsbury's; see Strategy).
  • Unverified That no refillable washable children's marker exists — no exhaustive market search.
  • Unverified That Crayola leads the UK market, or that Super Tips are "the standard" in UK classrooms — no reliable evidence found.
  • Hypothesis Classroom money saved, refill margin, refills per marker and waste reduction — need prices, usage tests and a pilot.
  • Hypothesis That a polypropylene body would be recyclable in practice — composition, separation and local recycling routes unchecked.

The Strategy page still lets you play with the price figures as clearly labelled assumptions, because the brief asks for reasonable estimates.

FAQ

Questions grown-ups might ask

Can I refill my Super Tips today?

No. This is a redesign concept, not a feature of the markers on sale, and this site does not explain how to open them. Please don't try.

Can I buy it?

No. There is no product, price or availability. The kit page is a pretend explorer.

Is it safe for children to refill?

No safety claim is made. The proposal is that only adults would handle ink and tips, and only if a safety evaluation supports it.

Will it reduce waste?

Unknown. It needs a life-cycle comparison of parts, packaging and transport. No figures are claimed.

Will the ink wash out?

Washability on skin and most washable clothing has not been tested for this design. Crayola describes its current Super Tips as washable [1]; that is its claim for the current product, not ours.

Who made this, and is Crayola involved?

It is an academic student project. It is not affiliated with, sponsored by or endorsed by Crayola.
